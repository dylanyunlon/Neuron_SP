## Abstract

Deep learning has advanced at an unprecedented pace. This progress has led to a significant increase in its complexity. However, despite extensive research on accelerating inference, training deep models directly within a resource-constrained budget remains a considerable challenge due to its high computational and memory requirements. In this paper, we introduce INSTANT (compressIng gradieNtS and acTivAtions for resource-efficieNt Training), a method designed to address both the computational and the memory bottlenecks when training. INSTANT reduces resource demands during backpropagation by projecting gradients and activations into a low-rank subspace and performing computation within that compressed representation. Experimental results demonstrate that INSTANT achieves a \$15\\times\$ reduction in computational cost and \$32\\times\$ reduction in activation memory with negligible impact on model performance. The code will be made publicly available upon the paper's acceptance.

#### Summary

The paper proposes a training-time compression method called INSTANT that projects activations and gradients to low-rank subspaces and performs backpropagation in these compressed representations. The authors describe three steps: (i) SVD-based calibration for constructing layer-wise projectors; (ii) projection/truncation based on energy threshold and oversampling; and (iii) low-rank backpropagation algorithm. Experiments show significant reduction in activation memory and FLOPs with small accuracy loss on Transformer and CNN models.

#### Strengths

1. Reliable empirical evaluation across different modalities (vision and NLP) and architectures (Transformer, CNN).
2. Clear presentation with reproducibility-focused appendix and pseudocode.
3. Practical significance: reduces activation memory and backward FLOPs through reasonable ablations (oversampling, calibration frequency, ordering choice).
4. Solid theoretical guarantees for stable low-rank training. Analysis shows SVD-based projection minimizes reconstruction error and gradient approximation error remains bounded in depth, approaching zero as retained energy \$\\varepsilon \\to 1\$.
5. Deployment-related experiments (edge device / Raspberry Pi).

#### Weaknesses

**1. Serious overlap with prior work (CompAct, NAACL 2025, available on arXiv since October 2024)**

Conceptually highly similar to CompAct [1] with parallel structure. Main difference lies in projection construction and calibration choices, with CompAct completely unmentioned despite being published months before the ICLR submission. Both papers compress activations via low-rank projection during forward pass, compute gradients in compressed subspace then decompress for weight update, aim to reduce both memory and optimizer overhead, and validate on LLaMA and BERT-like models.

INSTANT's algorithmic structure (forward compression → compressed backward → decompression for update) mirrors CompAct's Algorithms 1–3. Overlap also includes terminology ("projected activations", "reduced optimizer states"), theoretical justification, and empirical evaluation.

**2. Overclaimed novelty and contribution**

Although INSTANT adds implementation improvements such as calibration-based rank selection and changes to projection matrix selection, these are engineering extensions rather than conceptual advances. The claim of being "first to jointly compress activations and gradients" is not accurate given the overlap with prior work.

**3. Lack of comparability**

No comparison with the most relevant work (e.g., GaLore [2]) or subsequent work (VeLORA [3], Grass [4], WeLore [5]). The reported baselines are insufficient to demonstrate novelty or superiority.

#### References

[1] CompAct: Compressed Activations for Memory-Efficient LLM Training — Shamshoum et al., NAACL 2025, arXiv:2410.15352v1

[2] GaLore: Memory-Efficient LLM Training by Gradient Low-Rank Projection — Zhao et al., ICML 2024, arXiv:2403.03507

[3] VeLORA: Memory-Efficient Training with Rank-1 Subspace Activations — Miles et al., NeurIPS 2024 Poster

[5] WeLore: We are Low-Rank Heroes for LLM Fine-tuning — Jaiswal et al., ICLR 2025, arXiv:2407.11239

#### Questions

1. Were the authors aware of CompAct (NAACL 2025) at the time of submission?
2. What specific conceptual or methodological innovations exist in INSTANT beyond the choice of projection matrix compared to CompAct?
3. Can a direct empirical comparison with CompAct be provided under matched conditions (same model, rank, dataset)?
4. How do the claimed FLOP reductions compare against a random projection baseline?

#### Ethics Details

The paper omitted citation of a highly similar prior work (CompAct), raising concerns about the validity of novelty claims and appropriateness of attribution. Simply searching "LLM activation compression" on Google surfaces CompAct near the top of results, making inadvertent omission unlikely. Despite excellent experiments and presentation, a strong rejection is recommended unless the authors provide a comprehensive empirical comparison with the highly overlapping work.

### Reviewer jz4Y

**Date:** 31 Oct 2025 *(modified: 12 Nov 2025)*

**Soundness:** 2 — Fair**Presentation:** 3 — Good**Contribution:** 3 — Good**Rating:** 6 — Marginally above the acceptance threshold. But would not mind if paper is rejected**Confidence:** 4 — Confident but not absolutely certain**Flag for Ethics Review:** No

#### Summary

This paper proposes a compression method called INSTANT. INSTANT projects the activations and gradients to lower ranks, based on analysis done at regular intervals during training. The authors show the benefits of compressing both the gradients and activations during training by showing reduction in FLOPs and training time (in some cases).

#### Strengths

1. The paper tackles both memory and computational bottlenecks. Activation compression for memory saving has been explored previously, but the idea of also compressing the activation gradient (\$g\_y\$) to reduce backpropagation FLOPs is a useful contribution.
2. The authors provide empirical evidence (Fig. 3) to support their intuition that activation gradients are inherently low-rank, justifying their compression approach.
3. INSTANT's SVD-based approach is data-driven, allowing it to generalize more effectively to other modalities, as demonstrated by its strong performance on NLP tasks (Table 2) where previous works (e.g., LBP-WHT) struggle.
4. The authors validate the method's usability by reporting significant wall-clock speedups (\$2\\times\$ to \$12.5\\times\$) on resource-constrained edge CPUs.

#### Weaknesses

The paper's primary weaknesses lie in the evaluation of its computational claims and the transparency of its overhead costs.

**Omission of Wall-Clock Training Time on GPUs:**

1. The paper's main results (Tables 1 & 2) were generated on an NVIDIA V100 GPU but only report FLOPs, not wall-clock training time.
   a) The authors' justification that "FLOPs are unaffected by implementation details" is a weak defense. A reduction in FLOPs does not guarantee a proportional reduction in training time, especially on GPUs. Modern deep learning libraries (like cuDNN) are highly optimized for large, dense matrix multiplications, and the low-rank operations introduced by INSTANT may not be as implementation-efficient, thus creating a bottleneck.
   b) The authors themselves admit a discrepancy in Appendix I, stating "The (\$12\\times\$) time reduction is not comparable to (\$17\\times\$) FLOP reduction," even on a CPU. This gap is likely to be even larger on a GPU.

**Cost and Nature of the "Static" Subspace:**

2. The term "static" subspace (used in the contribution list and abstract) could be misleading. The subspace is not fixed; it is "periodically" recalibrated every \$N\_t\$ steps (e.g., \$N\_t = 50\$ or \$N\_t = 200\$).
   a) The computational cost of this recalibration (Algorithm 1) appears to be non-trivial and is not accounted for in the reported per-step FLOP savings. This calibration requires running multiple batches and performing SVD for every layer being compressed.
   b) The method's stability relies on an "oversampling" hyperparameter \$p\$, which is introduced to "reduce information loss when the core bases change" between calibrations. This suggests the "static" assumption is fragile and introduces another sensitive hyperparameter.

#### Questions

1. Could the authors please provide the total wall-clock training time (not just backward time) for the main V100 GPU experiments in Tables 1 and 2?
2. How is the computational cost of the periodic calibration step (Algorithm 1) factored into the total FLOPs reported? Could you provide an analysis of this overhead, for instance, as a percentage of total training time or FLOPs?
3. Given that the subspace is recalibrated every \$N\_t\$ steps, would "periodically updated" or "cached" be a more accurate description than "static"?
4. The oversampling parameter \$p\$ appears critical for performance (Fig. 6, Fig. 8). How sensitive is the method to the choice of \$p\$ and the calibration frequency \$N\_t\$? Does this introduce a significant hyperparameter tuning burden?
5. Following up on the \$17\\times\$ FLOP vs. \$12\\times\$ time gap noted in Appendix I: What is the authors' hypothesis for the performance gap on a V100, where highly optimized dense matrix multiplication kernels are the standard?

### Addressing Ethics Concerns: INSTANT vs. CompAct (Response 1/n)

First, we thank you for your detailed review. Our work is ​**completely original and does not contain any duplication, plagiarism, or academic misconduct of any kind**​.

***→ W1 & Q2 & "Ethics Issues": Serious overlap with prior work (CompAct, NAACL 2025)***

Our work is related to CompAct [1] in that both follow a similar research direction of using compression to address computational and memory bottlenecks during training. The mathematical distinction between CompAct and INSTANT in backpropagation:

**CompAct:** \$\\hat{g}\_W = K\_s^\\top \\cdot \\hat{x}\$ and \$g\_x = K\_s \\cdot W\$

**INSTANT:** \$g\_W = (K\_s^\\top \\cdot P^\\top) \\cdot (P \\cdot x)\$ and \$\\tilde{g}\_x = Q^\\top \\cdot ((Q \\cdot K\_s) \\cdot W)\$

INSTANT compresses the equations for computing both weight-activation gradients and activation gradients, dramatically reducing computational cost during backpropagation. **Our work does NOT integrate optimizer states.** Therefore, our work differs from all work that compresses optimizer states (including CompAct).

| Aspect             | CompAct                                            | INSTANT (ours)                                                     |
| -------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Compression target | Activations + weight gradients + optimizer states  | **Activations + activation gradients only**                  |
| Backpropagation    | Only weight gradient computation in low-rank space | **Both weight and activation gradients in compressed space** |
| Source of savings  | Optimizer state and activation memory              | **Backward FLOP reduction + activation memory**              |
| Projection         | Heuristic                                          | **Calibration-based low-rank subspace with proofs**          |

### Detailed Differences from CompAct (Response 2/n)

***→ "Computing gradients in compressed subspace then decompressing for weight update":***

There are two main matrix multiplications in backpropagation: \$g\_w\$ for weight update and \$g\_x\$ for propagating to the previous layer. CompAct computes only \$g\_w\$ in low-rank space while keeping \$g\_x\$ in original dimensions (as shown in CompAct Figure 2 and Algorithm 2). In contrast, ​*INSTANT performs both multiplications in low-rank space, dramatically reducing computation during backpropagation*​, as shown in our Equations (4) and (5).

***→ "Aiming to reduce both memory and optimizer overhead":***

This paper focuses only on reducing memory and computational cost during backpropagation, ​**without modifying optimizer states**​, unlike CompAct (and GaLore). While our low-rank projection mechanism could in principle be applied to optimizers, that is outside the scope of this work.

*Our work exploits the low-rank property of activation gradients.* This is a novel aspect not considered in other related works (CompAct, GaLore, WeLore, VeLORA, Grass).

***→ Q1: Were the authors aware of CompAct at submission?***

We were aware of it at submission but viewed it as a variant of GaLore in the gradient compression direction, already discussed in our related work. Our work was developed independently and concurrently. Failing to include a more detailed comparison with CompAct was **the only issue** regarding this matter. We have since revised the paper to add more references and clarify differences *(main paper lines 101–110, 267–269)* and expanded our baselines in Appendix H.

### Novelty and Random Projection Baseline (Response 3/n)

***→ W2.1: "The claim of being 'first to jointly compress activations and gradients' is inaccurate"***

**We are the first to exploit the low-rank property of activation gradients** across many data distributions, which has not been explored in previous works on compressing weights (WeLore), activations (CompAct, VeLORA), or optimizer states and weight gradients (CompAct, GaLore, Grass).

***→ W2.2: "Implementation improvements are engineering extensions, not conceptual advances"***

Our two baseline methods, Gradient Filtering and LBP-WHT, although compressing activations and activation gradients, are based on a low-frequency assumption of input data and only work well on image tasks. Our calibration-based rank selection strategy is innovative in that it ​**identifies an appropriate low-rank subspace for both activations and activation gradients, built on a mathematical foundation of sufficient statistics (Section 3.2) with clear proofs (Appendix B, C)**​.

On GPU V100, ​**INSTANT's backward time is \$1.4\\times\$ compared to Vanilla**​. The total training time is slightly higher than Vanilla. In short, INSTANT's FLOP reductions are not converted into time reduction on GPU.

***→ Q5: Hypothesis for the \$17\\times\$ FLOP vs. \$12\\times\$ time gap on V100***

**CUDA optimized for large matrices** is the main reason for the slower INSTANT implementation on GPU. A GPU with multiple cores is designed for parallel computations of large operations. Splitting a large operation into three smaller ones lowers the utilization of GPU cores and leads to overhead.

**Kernel launching overhead and memory bandwidth:** INSTANT's low-rank forward \$y = (x \\cdot P^\\top) \\cdot (P \\cdot w^\\top)\$ launches 3 CUDA kernels for 3 multiplications, while Vanilla \$y = x \\cdot w^\\top\$ requires launching only 1 CUDA kernel. Moreover, specialized memory types like GDDR6 or HBM lead to rapid data transfers, which reduces the time for large matrix loading.

**Why INSTANT gains on CPU:** Different from parallel computing on GPU with multiple cores, a CPU has a much smaller number of cores and cannot compute a large operation at once. Small matrices can be loaded from cache, which reduces time, while loading a large matrix from memory takes additional time.

We updated our paper *(line 431, line 1263)* to include this discussion.

### Calibration Overhead and Choices of Hyperparameters (Response 2/n)

***→ W2 & Q2: "How is the computational cost of the periodic calibration step factored into the total FLOPs?"***

Calibration is efficient: **does not increase peak memory** thanks to on-policy processing with smaller batch setup ​*(line 766, main paper)*​, and **small calibration computation** thanks to long-term subspace utilization.

**Proof of small computation overhead of calibration** (\$N\_t = 200\$, 5 calibration iterations per update, linear layer \$y = x \\cdot w^\\top\$ with \$x \\in \\mathbb{R}^{B \\times L \\times C\_x}\$, \$w \\in \\mathbb{R}^{C\_y \\times C\_x}\$):

1. Training FLOPs: \$\\text{flop}\_t = 200 \\cdot (6 \\cdot B \\cdot L \\cdot C\_x \\cdot C\_y)\$
2. Calibration FLOPs: \$\\text{flop}\_c = 5 \\cdot (6 \\cdot B \\cdot L \\cdot C\_x \\cdot C\_y) + 5 \\cdot (2 \\cdot B \\cdot L^2 \\cdot (C\_x + C\_y)) + [4/3 \\cdot L^3 + 4/3 \\cdot L^3]\$
3. Ratio: \$\\text{flop}\_c / \\text{flop}\_t \\approx \\frac{1}{40}\$ (when \$L < \\min(C\_x, C\_y)\$)

Therefore, calibration FLOPs are small compared to training FLOPs. For example, INSTANT-5 with 475 MFLOPs training cost *(line 351, main paper)* requires only \~37 additional MFLOPs for calibration. We added this analysis to the main paper ​*(line 419, line 810)*​.

***→ Q3: Would "periodically updated" be more accurate than "static"?***

Yes, "periodically updated" is more accurate. Our purpose of writing "static" was to indicate the slow change of the compression tensor over time (static over \$N\_t = 200\$ training iterations). We have changed this in the main text.

***→ Q4: Sensitivity to \$p\$ and calibration frequency \$N\_t\$***

**Oversampling \$p\$:** With small \$p\$ (0, 1, 2, 3), subspace selection varies significantly and performance changes considerably. When \$p\$ becomes larger (e.g., \$p = 5\$ vs \$p = 10\$), performance saturates since additional vectors no longer capture more of the core subspace. INSTANT is sensitive to \$p\$ when \$p\$ is small, but if the resource budget is larger, \$p\$ is not so sensitive.

**Calibration frequency \$N\_t\$:** Smaller \$N\_t\$ (more frequent updates) is better, as shown in the Appendix. However, smaller \$N\_t\$ results in more calibration overhead. To find an appropriate calibration frequency, we conducted an empirical study as shown in Table 1 ​*(line 340)*​, Table 2 *(line 378)* and Figure 8 ​*(line 1095)*​. The choices \$p = 5\$, \$N\_t = 200\$ perform well across 5 image datasets.

### Restatement of Existing Experiments and Extra Baselines (Response 1/n)

We would like to kindly point out that our paper presents a compression method that projects gradients and activations into a low-rank subspace and performs computation within these compressed representations. We do not perform distillation or grafting.

Regarding LBP-WHT, we refer the reviewer to Section 4 *(line 270, main paper)* for a detailed comparison in terms of both accuracy and computational efficiency ​*(Table 1, line 340 and Table 2, line 378)*​.

### Complex Datasets, Larger Models, and Calibration Overhead (Response 2/n)

***→ W2 & Q3: Limited experimental setup and more complex datasets***

In our experimental setup, we also conduct extensive experiments on BERT-like models using the GLUE benchmark ​*(Table 2, line 378)*​. These benchmarks are widely adopted in the community for assessing compression methods.

Fine-tuning EfficientFormer-L1 on Places-365 (1.8M images, more challenging than ImageNet):

| Method              | Accuracy        | Backward MFLOPs | Activation Memory |
| --------------------- | ----------------- | ----------------- | ------------------- |
| Vanilla             | 55.30           | 1484            | 1.95              |
| Gradient Filtering  | 9.2             | 24              | 0.04              |
| LBP-WHT-2           | 50.92           | 95              | 0.12              |
| LBP-WHT-4           | 53.27           | 335             | 0.40              |
| LBP-WHT-8           | 54.67           | 1227            | 1.43              |
| INSTANT-0           | 54.32           | 388             | 0.37              |
| **INSTANT-5** | **54.57** | **567**   | **0.60**    |
| INSTANT-7           | 54.55           | 606             | 0.66              |

All three INSTANT variants show only a minor reduction in performance compared to vanilla training, despite achieving a \~\$3\\times\$ reduction in both computational cost and memory. Gradient Filtering fails to converge. These experiments are added to the main paper ​*(Appendix L.3, line 1397)*​.

***→ W4 & Q2: Scaling to larger models***

In ​*Appendix L (line 1373)*​, we conduct additional experiments on larger models such as ViT-B/32 *(line 1385)* and TinyLlama ​*(line 1433)*​. On ViT-B/32, INSTANT achieves performance comparable to standard training while reducing both activation memory and computational cost by a factor of 5. For TinyLlama, the performance gap is larger, but our method still yields substantial savings.

Potential challenges for future work:

**Error accumulation in deeper models:** As model depth increases, error stacking may become more pronounced, necessitating more frequent calibration updates or a larger energy threshold and oversampling factor.

**Limited calibration for very large models:** As shown in ​*Appendix L.4 (line 1434)*​, when scaling to models with \~1B parameters, the performance gap widens. A small number of calibration batches may not provide sufficiently rich statistics to construct an accurate low-rank projection.

***→ W3.1: Computational cost of SVD***

Under our calibration scheme, SVD is performed only during the calibration phase and applied at most once per layer per calibration. The calibration cost is \$\\approx \\frac{1}{40}\$ of backpropagation cost (see proof above). The details are in ​*Appendix B.3 (line 762)*​.
